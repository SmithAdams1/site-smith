Why Portugal, in 2026

Portugal's appeal to foreign capital rests on a small set of facts that are easy to verify and hard to argue with:

  • An improving sovereign. Portugal's credit rating was raised through 2025: S&P to A in February and A+ in August, and Fitch to A in September, on sustained debt reduction and budget surpluses. (Source: S&P and Fitch, 2025.)
  • Safety. Portugal ranked 7th safest country in the world, and 5th in Europe, in the 2025 Global Peace Index.
  • EU, Eurozone and Schengen. Investment is made and returns are earned in euros, inside the single market, with transparent title.
  • Tourism-driven rental demand. Lisbon remains one of Europe's strongest urban hospitality markets, with year-round demand and a limited supply of professionally managed premium stays.

These are third-party markers, not our own numbers. They are the reason a Portugal allocation is a question of which asset, not whether the country.

From gross to net: what most Portugal numbers leave out

A gross yield is not a result. The return an investor actually keeps is what remains after the costs of buying and the costs of holding. A serious model prices in each of the following, and confirms every figure against the current year's law before relying on it.

  • IMT (property transfer tax), on purchase. For a second home on the mainland, IMT is progressive, from 1% on the first euros up to a top marginal 8%, becoming a flat 6% above roughly €621,500 and a flat 7.5% above roughly €1.13M (2025 bands; the 2026 bands were uplifted about 2%). A significant 2026 change specifically affects this article's readers: under Decree-Law 97/2026, non-tax-residents are reported to pay a flat 7.5% IMT on residential purchases regardless of value, with carve-outs (for example a refund if you become tax-resident within 24 months). This is recent legislation and the exact entry-into-force has been reported inconsistently, so treat the 7.5% non-resident rate as current-but-confirmable and check it against the Diário da República or a Portuguese tax adviser before you budget on it. (Sources: Portal das Finanças, CIMT 2025/2026; Decree-Law 97/2026.)
  • Stamp Duty (Imposto do Selo), on purchase: 0.8% of price or taxable value (2025/2026, unchanged).
  • IMI (annual municipal property tax): 0.3% to 0.45% of the taxable value (VPT) for urban property, set by each municipality (2025/2026).
  • AIMI (wealth surcharge on higher-value property): exempt below €600,000 of VPT per individual, then 0.7% (to €1M), 1.0% (€1M to €2M) and 1.5% above €2M (2025/2026).
  • Rental income tax: a 25% autonomous rate on residential letting for non-residents; for residents, a 2026 "moderate rent" regime taxes leases with rent up to €2,300/month and a term of at least three years at 10% (2026 to 2029), otherwise 25%. (Source: PwC Tax Summaries; OE 2026.)
  • Capital gains, on sale: since 2023, residents and non-residents are treated alike, with 50% of the gain taxable at progressive IRS rates (the old 28% flat rate for non-residents on the full gain was abolished as discriminatory). (Source: PwC Tax Summaries, 2025/2026.)
  • Management: for professionally managed short-term rental, commonly around 21% of gross rental revenue.

Stacked together, these costs mean the net-of-cost yield an owner keeps is materially below the gross figures on the chart above, often by a third or more once tax and management are counted. That is not a reason to avoid Portugal; it is the reason to model the specific asset rather than buy a headline yield. Residency, if that is part of the plan

Many international buyers pair a Portugal investment with a residency route. Two facts are essential and current as of 2026:

  • The direct real-estate purchase route to the Golden Visa closed in October 2023. Buying an apartment no longer qualifies on its own. Current qualifying routes are non-real-estate: regulated CMVM investment funds (commonly from €500,000, with the fund at least 60% invested in Portuguese companies), cultural heritage support (from €250,000), eligible lower-density investment (from €200,000), scientific research, and business or job creation. Administered by AIMA.
  • The D2 visa is a separate, older route for entrepreneurs and the self-employed, and can suit investors building or running a business in Portugal.

We cover each in its own guide (published alongside this one).

Tax in 2026: NHR is gone

The single most common mistake in older Portugal content is describing the classic Non-Habitual Resident (NHR) regime as available. It is not.

  • NHR closed to new applicants on 1 January 2025 and was replaced by the IFICI regime, often called "NHR 2.0", the Tax Incentive for Scientific Research and Innovation.
  • IFICI offers a 20% flat rate on eligible Portuguese employment or self-employment income for ten years, plus relief on certain foreign income, but its eligibility is narrow: highly-qualified roles (a degree at EQF level 6 or above, or a PhD) in science, technology and innovation, healthcare, green energy, R&D, and qualifying startups or exporters, and only for people who were not Portuguese tax residents in the prior five years. It is not automatic for a passive property investor.

For non-residents, a fiscal representative is generally required, which is one of the things Smith & Adams handles through its Tax Representation practice.

Where investors are buying

Portugal is not one market. Each of these has a different risk, yield and buyer profile:

  • Lisbon: the deepest rental demand and the strongest short-term-rental market, at premium pricing and, as a result, the lowest gross yield (4.3% in Q2 2026).
  • Porto: lower entry pricing than Lisbon with a maturing rental market, and a slightly higher gross yield (4.8%).
  • Algarve: seasonal tourism and second-home demand; the Faro district capital showed 4.8% gross in Q2 2026, though holiday-let hotspots behave differently and season heavily.
  • Comporta and the Alentejo coast: scarce, high-end and lifestyle-led, with no published residential yield series; here the case is scarcity and capital preservation rather than a quoted yield.

Frequently asked questions

Is Portugal still a good place to invest in property in 2026?
As of 2026 Portugal combines an upgraded sovereign credit rating (S&P A+, Fitch A in 2025), EU and euro membership, and strong tourism-driven rental demand. It remains attractive, but the decision should turn on the specific asset and its net-of-cost return, not the country alone.

What rental yield can I expect in Portugal?
Gross yields as of Q2 2026 were about 4.3% in Lisbon, 4.8% in Porto and 4.8% in Faro, against a 6.2% national average (idealista, asking rent over asking price). These are gross figures; the net yield after taxes, management and vacancy is materially lower, and should always be modelled on the specific asset.

Can I still get a Golden Visa by buying property in Portugal?
No. The direct real-estate purchase route closed in October 2023. As of 2026 the Golden Visa qualifies through non-real-estate routes such as regulated investment funds (commonly from €500,000), cultural heritage (from €250,000) and eligible lower-density investment (from €200,000), administered by AIMA.

Is the NHR tax regime still available?
No. NHR closed to new applicants on 1 January 2025 and was replaced by IFICI, which has narrow eligibility focused on highly-qualified roles in science, technology, healthcare, green energy and R&D. It is not automatic for passive property investors.

What does "net-of-cost return" mean?
It is the return that remains after acquisition costs, annual property taxes, management fees and income tax, expressed in euros. Smith & Adams reports performance net of cost, quarterly, on every asset it manages.
